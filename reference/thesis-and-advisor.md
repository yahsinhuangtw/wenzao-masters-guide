## Choosing a topic (coaching workflow)

Run this as a short interview, and don't write the topic for the student. In your first reply ask at most two things: what they keep reading or thinking about (offer a few example directions if they have already named an interest), and which languages they can read sources in. Don't ask for the department yet: you need it only when you get to forms, deadlines, or past theses. Also tell them, in one line, that past theses in their department are a good way to see what a finished topic looks like, and offer to point to them once you know the department. Ask the rest one or two at a time in later turns.

1. **Interests.** What are two or three things you keep reading or thinking about?
2. **Why it matters.** Why these? A personal reason is a good start, it is what carries you through a year of work.
3. **Resources you have.** Which languages can you read sources in? What can you access (people, documents, data, a home-country context)? How much time do you really have? Which courses have you taken that relate?
4. **Narrow it.** Turn the interest into a question, then test it three ways:
   - *Answerable:* do sources exist that can answer it?
   - *Feasible:* can you do it in the time you have, in the languages you can read, with access you can get?
   - *Supervisable:* is there a faculty member whose work is close enough?
5. **Check what's been done.** Look at past theses in the department (links in the department file) to see scope and style, and to avoid repeating one.
6. **Write it down.** Fill in `templates/thesis-topic-worksheet.md` with the student, and keep it to one page.

For international students, a home-country context or language skills (reading sources in languages local students can't) can be a real research advantage, so ask about it.

## Choosing an advisor

1. Make a shortlist of two or three faculty from the department's faculty page: look at their research interests and read one or two of their publications.
2. If you can, talk to current students about what it is like to work with each of them.
3. Send a short email (`templates/advisor-email.md`, English and Mandarin versions). Say who you are, what you are interested in, what you have read of theirs, and ask for a short meeting.
4. At the first meeting, use `templates/advisor-meeting-agenda.md`. Ask about expectations: how often you will meet, what language you will communicate in, how long feedback usually takes, and what the timeline to the proposal looks like.
5. If it isn't a fit, it is normal to ask someone else. Do it early, before the proposal.

Taiwanese universities commonly address faculty as "老師" (teacher) plus surname, or by title. Use a polite, formal tone in the first email. This is custom, not a rule, so a native speaker should check any Mandarin before sending.

## Working with your advisor

- Send a short agenda before each meeting and a two-line summary of what was agreed after it.
- Bring something concrete: a paragraph, an outline, a list of sources.
- Keep a simple log of meeting dates and next steps, it helps when you plan the proposal.

## AI use

Before any help with thesis writing, check the department's AI-use guidelines (`departments/dia.md` links DIA's, GIES has none known yet, `TODO(verify)`). Coach and structure, don't write the thesis. If the guidelines are unclear for a particular use, tell the student to ask their advisor.

## What the agent should do

- If the student has no topic: run the topic workflow.
- If the student has a topic and no advisor: help with the shortlist and the email.
- If the student has an advisor: help prepare meetings, and check the calendar for the next proposal or oral exam deadline.
- Always remind the student that the application deadline in the calendar (`2027-01-08`, `2027-07-09`) may not be the only deadline, and that the department office confirms the real steps.
