Abstract
Nanwang Puyuma is spoken in Nanwang, Taitung. Drawing on extracts from a short film that spanning nearly four decades, this talk examines the gradual disappearance of shamanism in the Nanwang Puyuma community. 
The Puyuma were traditionally a shamanistic society. During the second half of the 20th century, however, this system underwent a profound transformation: from a society in which shamanism was an integral part of social and ritual life, it became a society with a relatively small number of practicing shamans, and today only one shaman remains. These transformation echo changes observed in many former shamanistic societies worldwide following their incorporation into, and sustained contact with, other communities. As Roberte Hamayon (1982:42) observes, shamanistic societies “only experience state centralisation through acculturation or the domination of a state-controlled society, or at the end of an internal evolution which results in the marginalization, transformation, or even disappearance of shamanism.” 
Through linguistic and audiovisual evidence, I examine the factors and mechanisms underlying this decline, with particular attention to the ways in which social historical, and linguistic changes have contributed to the erosion of shamanic practices.
In particular, I will discuss the significance of Puyuma shamanistic ritual texts that have been preserved through my own documentation (Cauquelin 2008), and consider what these texts can reveal about a ritual tradition that is now approaching extinction.
